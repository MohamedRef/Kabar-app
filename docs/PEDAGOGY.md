# Kabar pedagogy — lesson and session standards

This is the reference for how Kabar teaches, how fast the curriculum moves and how a day of study is built. It covers one adult learner (Egyptian Arabic native, reads English) studying Indonesian (BIPA 1–7) and then Javanese (J1–J6) alone on a phone, a tablet and a Mac. **No study tells you how long a language-app lesson should be.** The caps below are design decisions. Each one is built on solid findings: spacing, retrieval practice, segmenting and working-memory limits. The specific numbers are synthesized from that evidence, and the doc labels them as such. They get recalibrated from real attempt times (§7).

Labels used throughout: **[synth]** = a number synthesized or inferred from the evidence, not measured. **[weak]** = indirect or weak evidence. Starred references (\*) are standard citations that were not re-checked during this research round, so verify exact figures before quoting them elsewhere.

### 1.1 The caps

| New grammar points | **≤ 2** (1 preferred) | ≤ 1 (a recap of the unit's grammar) [synth] | 0 |
| In-lesson retrievals per new word | **≥ 3**, across ≥ 2 exercise types | ≥ 3 | n/a |

| Daily plan (*Rencana Hari Ini*) | Reviews | New lesson work | Other | New words/day | New grammar/day |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

| **15 min** (default) | ~6 min (≥ 40%) | 1 lesson (~6 min) | weak spot ~3 min | ≤ 12 | ≤ 2 (lesson cap) |

| **Comeback after a break** | light 5-minute plan | — | — | as 5 min | none |

The 30-minute plan is two 15-minute blocks, with a short break offered in between.

These sit **outside** the lesson caps and have their own limits: mini-games; Kabar Radio (≤ 8 min, already enforced by the validator's 3–8 minute rule); Kabar Pagi (2 min); level tests (≤ 30 min, split into sections).

### 1.2 Why, in five lines
1. **Spacing beats set size.** Many short, spaced encounters beat one long session [10][12][18][20][21]. So lessons are short, and most repetition goes to the review engine.
2. **Retrieval beats re-reading** [19][23][24]. Exercises outnumber slides about 3:1, and every new word is retrieved at least 3 times before the lesson ends [26].
3. **Passive input loses people after about 6 minutes** [1]. Segmenting helps, with small to medium effects [6]. A lesson capped at 7 minutes is a single segment.
4. **Working memory holds about 4 chunks** [7]. That is the reason for ≤ 3 examples per slide, few grammar points, and Javanese ngoko/krama pairs taught as one item.
5. **Shortening lessons too far backfired at Duolingo** [30], and *lessons completed* predicts gains better than minutes do [29]. That sets the 4-minute floor and is why progress is counted in lessons.

## 2. Evidence

### 2.1 Microlearning and attention: short segments, not short days

The best behavioural data comes from Guo, Kim & Rubin. They looked at 6.9 million edX video sessions and found that **median engagement tops out at about 6 minutes, however long the video is**. Videos of 0–3 minutes kept viewers best. Their recommendation was "segment videos into short chunks, ideally less than 6 minutes" [1]. The data is correlational and measures watch time, not learning. Still, it is the closest real-world figure for how long people stay with continuous passive content. Two widely quoted limits do not hold up. The "10-minute lecture attention span" rests on thin evidence and varies with teacher, task and learner [2][3]. The "8-second attention span" goes back to a marketing report with no primary study behind it [4]. Microlearning reviews generally report positive retention effects, but they define "micro" as anything from about 2 to 15 minutes, and many of the studies are small or quasi-experimental [5] [weak]. **Nobody has run a controlled trial comparing 5-, 10- and 15-minute mobile L2 lessons on retention.** The evidence supports segments of roughly 2–6 minutes inside a longer day. It does not support a short day.

### 2.2 Cognitive load and segmenting: about 4 chunks at a time

Adult working memory holds **about 4 ± 1 chunks** (Cowan), not Miller's 7 ± 2 [7]. A segmenting meta-analysis (56 investigations, 88 comparisons) found **small to medium gains in retention and transfer**. It also found that segmenting adds learning time [6], so the time model must count transitions. Cognitive-load work separates low-interactivity material, such as isolated words, from high-interactivity material. Indonesian meN-/di- affixation and Javanese speech levels are high-interactivity, because several elements have to be processed together [8]. Expertise reversal means scaffolding that helps a beginner gets in the way later, so hints should fade as accuracy rises [9]. No study gives a validated "items per slide" figure for language apps. **≤ 3 examples per slide (one rule plus up to 3 examples ≈ 4 chunks) is [synth]** from capacity research.

### 2.3 Vocabulary load and retrievals: spacing over set size, at least 3 retrievals

Studying 20 words together was no worse than studying them in sets of 4 or 10 when spacing was held equal. **Spacing mattered more than set size** [10]. One large spaced stack of flashcards beat several massed small stacks for about 90% of learners, and most of those learners wrongly believed massing worked better [12]\*. Nakata (2017) compared 1, 3, 5 and 7 within-session retrievals. **5–7 retrievals scored higher than 1–3 on delayed tests. But per unit of time, a single retrieval gave the largest gain** [11]. That trade-off favours a few retrievals inside the lesson and the rest across later days. Successive-relearning research points the same way. **About 3 correct recalls at first** has diminishing returns beyond that once spaced relearning sessions follow [26]\*. Incidental learning from reading needs about 10 or more encounters, with no magic number [13]\*[14]\*. Teaching semantically tight sets together (all colours, all opposites) slows learning through interference. Loose thematic grouping, by situation or topic, does not [15]\*[16]\*[17]\*. No validated words-per-day ceiling exists for adults. **The 3–8 words per lesson and ≤ 12/≤ 20 per day are [synth].** They follow from the retrieval budget and from the review debt that each new word creates.

### 2.4 Spacing, retrieval and interleaving: the review engine does most of the work

A widely cited review rates **practice testing and distributed practice as the two "high utility" techniques** [19]\*. Cepeda's meta-analysis (317 experiments) shows spaced practice beating massed practice [20]\*. The best gap between study sessions is a fraction of the retention interval: roughly 20–40% for a week, shrinking to about 5–10% for a year. **A gap that is too long hurts less than one that is too short** [21]\*. Bahrick's 5-year vocabulary study found that 13 sessions 56 days apart roughly matched 26 sessions 14 days apart [22]\*. Retrieval beats restudy on delayed tests [23]\*. Dropping words from testing after the first correct recall cut 1-week recall from about 80% to about 35% [24]\*. Harder retrievals at longer lags, as long as they succeed, help more [25]\*. Whether the spacing schedule expands or stays even matters less than the total amount of spacing [53]\*[54]\*. In Serrano's review of 47 L2 studies, spacing beat massing for vocabulary, which she calls robust. Interleaved or hybrid grammar practice tended to beat blocked practice over the long term [18]. Kim & Webb's meta-analysis (37 studies) confirms spacing effects in L2 learning [52]\*. **Interleaving is not universal.** In the interleaving meta-analysis the overall effect was g = 0.42, but **for word learning it was g = −0.39: blocking was better** (few studies) [27]. Kabar therefore introduces new words in a block, then mixes them with older words in review. It interleaves grammar contrasts such as meN- vs di- and ngoko vs krama.

The "85% rule" says learning is fastest at about 15% error. It comes from simulations of gradient-descent learners, not from human L2 studies [28]\*. **A target of 80–90% first-try accuracy is [weak].** It is a sensible band for tuning difficulty, not a finding.

### 2.5 What apps do: 5–15 minutes, small word batches, lessons counted

| App | Published lesson / daily size | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Duolingo | No official minutes per lesson. Internal target **15+ min/day**. **Shorter lessons hurt learning time** in its own experiments. Intermediate mini-units add "a handful of new words". About 800 words per CEFR level (A1, A2) | [30][31][32] |
| Babbel | "10 to 15 minute lessons", one a day | [34] |
| Busuu | "10 minutes a day" | [36] |
| LingoDeer | ~5 min, **5–7 new words** per lesson (3rd-party) | [37] |
| Memrise | ~5-min sessions, 5 new words by default (configurable) (3rd-party) | [38] |
| Drops | 5 min/day free tier | [39] |
| Pimsleur | ~30-min audio lesson, one a day (3rd-party) | [40] |
| Airlearn (Unacademy) | grammar slides → immediate practice, units "in just a few minutes" (3rd-party) | [41] |

The most useful single data point comes from Duolingo's blog: **"making lessons shorter to encourage more of them" ultimately hurt time spent learning well** [30]. Lessons have a useful floor as well as a ceiling. In an independent study of 287 adult Duolingo learners over 6 months, **lessons completed was the most consistent predictor of gains and total minutes was not**. The effects were small (about 6% of variance), and the study was correlational with attrition [29]. Babbel and Busuu efficacy claims tie gains to hours (e.g. 75% improved at least one ACTFL sublevel after 15+ hours) [35]. Those studies are company-funded and had about 36% attrition [weak]. Duolingo's switch to half-life-regression spaced review raised daily retention by 12% in an A/B test [33].

### 2.6 Study hours: CEFR, BIPA, FSI

Cambridge gives cumulative guided learning hours of **A1 ~90–100, A2 ~180–200, B1 ~350–400, B2 ~500–600**, about 200 hours per step, "as a guideline only" [42]. Instituto Cervantes gives hours *per level*: **90 / 120 / 150 / 180 h for A1–B2**. Each level is longer than the one before [43]. Goethe and Alliance Française give similar ranges [55].

The official BIPA standard (Permendikbud 27/2017) defines 7 levels and **sets no hours** [44]. Universities set their own. UNMAS Denpasar requires **at least 100 hours per level** [45]. UNJ gives **272 lesson hours per pair of levels**, which is about 136 lesson hours per level, or about 102 clock hours if a lesson hour is 45 minutes. UNJ does not state the length [46]. The government *Sahabatku Indonesia* BIPA 1 book has 10 thematic units [47]. FSI is reported to place Indonesian in Category III, **36 weeks / 900 class hours** to professional proficiency. That figure comes from a secondary source [48] [weak]. DLI basic courses run 36–64 weeks [49]. Javanese has almost no hours data. ANU teaches it at 3 hours a week and requires Indonesian first [50]. UM runs a 32-hour conversation course [51]. **All of these figures assume English or European native speakers. None is for an Arabic speaker**, who has a head start from Arabic loanwords (see §4.6).

## 3. The standards

These are normative. The content validator enforces them (§6).

### 3.1 Lesson kinds and caps

**Every lesson: estimated time ≤ 7 minutes, target 4–6.** The 7-minute ceiling keeps a lesson to about one Guo-sized segment of attention [1]. The 4-minute floor guards against the Duolingo finding that lessons cut too short lose learning [30]. A 6-minute lesson fits the 15-minute default as one block, next to reviews and a weak spot.

**learn** lessons introduce new material.
- **1–4 slides, each with ≤ 3 examples.** Slides are the passive part. Four slides at the maximum come to about 4 minutes of the 7, which already leaves too little room for retrieval, so in practice 2–3 slides. One slide is enough when a lesson is the second half of a split topic. Three examples plus the rule is about 4 chunks [7].
- **6–10 exercises using ≥ 4 types.** Exercises are the retrieval budget [19][23]. Six is the minimum that gives every new word 3 retrievals when some exercises (match_pairs, build_sentence) cover several words. Ten is about where the 7-minute ceiling bites. Four or more types means each word is met as recognition, recall and listening, not the same format over and over. Varied formats also make each retrieval harder in a useful way [25] [synth].
- **Dialogue 4–6 lines.** About 30–50 seconds. Every new item appears in context at least once, which counts towards the ≥ 10 encounters that full knowledge needs [13][14].
- **3–8 new words.** Three makes a lesson worth opening. Eight × 3 retrievals = 24 retrieval events, which fits 6–10 exercises. This matches commercial batches of 5–7 [37][38] and the "spacing over set size" result [10] [synth].
- **Core words are taught before they are used.** A word in the top 300 of the frequency list (`content/freq/<lang>.tsv`) that an exercise uses must be a `new_words` entry in that lesson or an earlier one. Otherwise it never reaches the review deck, which only schedules taught words. The content check enforces this; `content/freq/<lang>.core-exclude.tsv` lists the few exceptions (names, glosses) and `core-pending.tsv` the rows still being fixed (#136).
- **≤ 2 grammar points (1 preferred).** Grammar is high-interactivity [8]. Two is allowed only when the second is a small companion to the first (e.g. *selamat* + time-of-day words).

**practice** lessons consolidate the unit.
- **0–1 recap slide; 6–10 exercises (≥ 4 types); dialogue 4–6 lines; ≤ 6 new words; ≤ 1 grammar point.** Practice exists so that items return at a lag inside the unit, before FSRS takes over [11][25]. Up to 6 new words lets the dialogue sound natural (the situational words of a market or a station) without turning the lesson into a learn lesson. **The one grammar point is [synth]**: it is the unit's grammar, recapped, not a new rule.

**review** lessons close the unit and carry the souvenir.
- **0–1 recap slide; 8–12 exercises (≥ 4 types); dialogue 4–6 lines; 0 new words.** This is a retrieval-only checkpoint [24][26] with interleaved grammar contrasts [18]. It gets more exercises because there is no teaching to fit in. The 7-minute time cap still applies.

The count caps alone do not guarantee the time cap. Four slides with 3 examples each (240 s) plus ten translate exercises (400 s) already passes 10 minutes. **The time estimate is the binding cap**, and the counts are guard rails.

This narrows SPEC §14's Airlearn-style pattern (3–6 slides → 8–15 exercises → dialogue → new words → souvenir) to fit the time cap. It keeps every element. A unit that needs more teaching gets **more lessons, not longer lessons**.

### 3.2 Time model (seconds)

| Item | Seconds | Item | Seconds |
|---|---|---|---|
| slide | 30 + 10 per example | build_sentence | 30 |
| multiple_choice | 15 | match_pairs | 35 |
| listen | 20 | dictation | 35 |
| fill_blank | 20 | translate | 40 |
| speaking | 20 | dialogue line | 8 |
| new-words summary | 10 + 3 per word | | |

**These are starting estimates [synth].** They come from rough reading and response times, not measurements, and are recalibrated from real attempt times (§7).

Worked examples:

| Lesson | Contents | Estimate |
|---|---|---|
| learn (typical) | 2 slides × 2 examples (100) · MC×2, listen, match_pairs, fill_blank, build_sentence, translate, speaking (195) · 4 lines (32) · 5 words (25) | 352 s → **6 min** |
| learn (lightest legal) | 2 slides × 1 example (80) · MC×3, listen, fill_blank, speaking (105) · 4 lines (32) · 3 words (19) | 236 s → **4 min** |
| practice | 0 slides · MC, listen, fill_blank, build_sentence, match_pairs, dictation, translate, speaking (215) · 5 lines (40) · 2 words (16) | 271 s → **5 min** |
| review | 1 recap slide × 2 (50) · MC×2, listen×2, fill_blank, build_sentence, match_pairs, dictation, translate, speaking (250) · 6 lines (48) | 348 s → **6 min** |

### 3.3 Daily plans (*Rencana Hari Ini*)

**5 minutes: reviews ≤ 2 min (≤ 12 cards) + the next part of a lesson.** *Lanjut* resumes the lesson tomorrow. The plan allows ≤ 5 new words and no new grammar unless it finishes a lesson. A 5-minute day is a real study day, not a failure. It keeps daily contact going, and logins and lessons completed are what predict gains [29]. Splitting a lesson across two days is a spaced repetition of its first half [20]. Reviews come first because a single retrieval gives the most per minute [11]. Twelve cards in 2 minutes is about 10 seconds per card [synth].

**15 minutes (default): reviews ~6 min (≥ 40%) + 1 new lesson (~6 min) + weak spot ~3 min; ≤ 12 new words.** Fifteen minutes is Duolingo's internal target and Babbel's framing [30][34]. Reviews at 40% or more keep FSRS debt from growing [19][21]. One lesson is one attention segment [1]. The weak spot targets the lowest-accuracy items, which is the "Review Manager" idea [34], driven by Kabar's own error data. The ≤ 12 cap allows one learn lesson's 8 words plus weak-spot or practice spill-over [synth].

**30 minutes: two 15-minute blocks, with a short break offered between them.** That comes to reviews ~12 min + 2 new lessons (~12 min) + weak spot ~3 min + reading/listening ~3 min. The caps are **≤ 20 new words and ≤ 2 new grammar points per day.** Two blocks keep each one within the segment limit [1][6]. The gap between them is a small spacing gap [21]. Twenty new words a day is the top of the 10–20 range that the review debt allows [synth]. Reading/listening (Reader, Kabar Pagi, a Sinetron scene) gives the incidental encounters that deliberate study cannot [13][14].

**Comeback after a break: a 5-minute light plan.** After a break, overdue reviews are capped and older items are re-introduced gently. Long gaps hurt less than people fear [21], and SPEC §6 rules out shame or streak drama.

### 3.4 Outside the lesson caps
- **Mini-games** (Pasar!, Rupiah Reader, Minimal Pairs, Sound-Shift Detective, Unggah-Ungguh Simulator): their length is set per game. They introduce no new words that are not already on the learner's cards [synth].
- **Kabar Radio**: ≤ 8 min. The validator already enforces 3–8. SPEC §14 says "5-minute", which sits inside that range.
- **Kabar Pagi**: 2 min.
- **Level tests / monthly check**: ≤ 30 min, split into sections that can each be resumed. The 10-minute monthly check from SPEC §6 is one such section.

## 4. Teaching method

### 4.1 Lesson flow
**Short slides → retrieval-heavy exercises → dialogue → new-words summary → souvenir** (review lessons only). Slides follow the worked-example principle: the rule plus up to 3 examples, then straight into practice. Exercises run from easy to hard: recognition (multiple_choice, match_pairs, listen) → guided recall (fill_blank, build_sentence) → free recall (translate, dictation, speaking). Hints fade as accuracy rises [9]. The dialogue puts everything back into a real situation. The summary is shown last, so it confirms what was retrieved instead of replacing the retrieval.

### 4.2 Retrieval schedule per new word
- **In the lesson: ≥ 3 retrievals across at least two exercise types**, with other trials between them. Never drill one word back to back [24][25][26].
- **Then the review engine: about 1 day, 3–7 days and 14–21 days**, then FSRS's own intervals [20][21][26]. The exact schedule shape matters less than the total spacing [53].
- A word missed in the lesson comes back once more before the lesson ends, and is flagged for the next weak spot.

### 4.3 Difficulty target
**Aim for about 80–90% first-try accuracy** on a lesson's exercises [28] [weak]. Over a rolling window: below about 75%, the coach adds a practice lesson and lowers new words. Above about 95%, it allows the full new-word cap and harder formats [synth]. The validator can't check this; the coach measures it.

### 4.4 Grouping vocabulary
**Group words by topic or situation, not by tight semantic sets** [15][16][17]. "At the warung" (*nasi*, *pedas*, *berapa*, *bungkus*) is fine. All the colours or all the kinship terms in one lesson is not. **Javanese ngoko/krama pairs are introduced as one item**: one card with form + register (*mangan* / *nedha*, eat). They are not two confusable words. The ngoko/krama choice is then practised by interleaving it in later lessons and in the Unggah-Ungguh Simulator [18][27].

### 4.5 Grammar
**≤ 1–2 new grammar points per lesson**, ≤ 2 per day. Teach with a worked example, then guided practice, then free production [8][9]. Contrasting grammar points (meN- vs di-, *sudah*/*belum*/*masih*) are interleaved in practice and review lessons, never blocked forever [18].

### 4.6 Arabic loans as Bridge anchors
Where a lesson's theme allows, at least one new word is an Arabic loan with the Bridge badge (*kabar* ← خبر, *selamat* ← سلامة, *waktu*, *kursi*). False friends (*kalimat* = sentence) get flagged on the spot. These are fast wins. **That loans are learned faster is an untested inference [weak].** The research notes found no study of Arabic-L1 learners of Indonesian.

## 5. Curriculum pacing

### 5.1 Fixed lesson length, growing lesson counts
Higher levels need more hours (Cervantes 90 → 120 → 150 → 180 h per level [43]; Cambridge's widening cumulative gaps [42]). Kabar keeps the **lesson length fixed (≤ 7 min) and gives each unit more lessons as levels rise.** Illustrative shape [synth]: BIPA 1 about 5 lessons per unit, rising by about one lesson per unit per level to about 10 at BIPA 6–7. Javanese follows the same pattern from J1. The unit structure stays as learn lessons → practice → review (souvenir).

> **Current shape (October 2026):** every lesson is ≤ 7 min, and lessons per unit grow with the level:
>
> | Level | BIPA 1 | BIPA 2 | BIPA 3 | BIPA 4 | BIPA 5 | BIPA 6 | BIPA 7 |
> |---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
> | Lessons per unit | 5–12 (74 in all) | 6–7 | 7 | 8 | 9 | 10 | 10 |
>
> | Level | J1 | J2 | J3 | J4 | J5 | J6 |
> |---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
> | Lessons per unit | 5–6 | 6 | 7 | 8 | 9 | 10 |

### 5.2 Weeks per level
The universities' working figure is **~100–136 contact hours per BIPA level** [45][46]. The table below is plain arithmetic, studying every day [synth]:

| Hours | 5 min/day | 15 min/day | 30 min/day |
|---|---|---|---|
| ~30–40 h (intro Javanese course size [50][51]) | ~51–69 wk | ~17–23 wk | ~9–11 wk |
| **100 h** (one BIPA level, UNMAS minimum) | ~171 wk | **~57 wk** | ~29 wk |
| **136 h** (one BIPA level, UNJ lesson hours) | ~233 wk | **~78 wk** | ~39 wk |
| ~200 h (≈ CEFR A2 cumulative) | ~343 wk | ~114 wk | ~57 wk |

The lesson spine runs on a different clock. At about 1 lesson a day (15 min) a 50-lesson level takes about 7 weeks. At 2 a day (30 min) it takes about 4 weeks. At about half a lesson a day (5 min) it takes about 14 weeks. That is far below the classroom-hour figures. **The conversion between app minutes and classroom hours is unknown.** No institution publishes one [42][44]. App study is denser: all retrieval, no waiting for others. But it lacks the free conversation and immersion that classroom hours include. So these tables are **planning ceilings, not forecasts.** The coach does not promise dates from them. It **recalibrates from real attempt times and level-check results** (SPEC §6 monthly check and forecast), and shows forecasts gently, without pressure.

## 6. How Kabar enforces it

- **Validator (`crates/content`).** Every lesson file in `content/lessons/**` is checked against §3.1 for its `kind`: slide count, examples per slide, exercise count, distinct exercise types, dialogue lines, new-word count and grammar-point count. It also checks that **estimated time ≤ 7 min** under the §3.2 model. If a lesson file breaks a cap, `just check` fails. The caps and time-model constants live in one place, `kabar_core::lesson::pacing`, so the validator, the Lesson Player and the coach use the same numbers. The ≥ 3 retrievals per new word is an authoring target; the validator does not check it yet, because exercise `words` lists are not complete enough to count on.
- **Curriculum `minutes`.** For each lesson in `curriculum/*.yaml`, `minutes` = **the time-model estimate rounded up to the next whole minute**. The validator flags any mismatch, so a hand-typed number can't drift away from the content. Lessons not written yet (BIPA 3–7, J4–J6) carry a placeholder of 6 minutes; every curriculum lesson must be ≤ 7.
- **Coach daily plan (`crates/core`).** The plan builder sizes *Rencana Hari Ini* to §3.3. The order is: reviews first (card cap and time share), then the next lesson or lesson part (resumable with *Lanjut*), then weak spot, then reading/listening at 30 min. It enforces the per-day new-word and grammar caps across all devices through the synced attempt log. After a break it offers the comeback plan.
- **Outside-cap content** (Radio, Pagi, games, tests) keeps its own validator rules (Radio 3–8 min already exists; tests ≤ 30 min in sections).
- **Fixtures and tests** use SPEC §24 test texts. Time-model tests pin the worked examples in §3.2.

## 7. Open questions / revisit

1. **Calibrate the time model after 2 weeks of real use.** Compare estimated seconds with median real attempt time per exercise type, per slide and per dialogue line. Update the constants, re-run the validator, and re-derive curriculum `minutes`. Expect beginner exercises to run slower and later ones faster.
2. **Is 80–90% first-try accuracy right for this learner?** [weak] Check it against FSRS retention after a month.
3. **Words-per-day caps (12 / 20)** [synth]. Watch the review forecast. If daily reviews in the 15-minute plan regularly go past ~6 minutes, lower intake.
4. **App-minutes ↔ classroom-hours conversion.** Unknown. Estimate it from monthly level-check gains vs time studied.
5. **Arabic-loan speed-up.** Compare retention of Bridge words with non-loans in the review log.
6. **Javanese pacing.** There is no hours standard for it. Revisit after J1.
7. **Speaking exercises** (later per SPEC): the 20 s estimate has no basis until speech grading exists.
